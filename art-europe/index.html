Editor’s letter

Every painting begins with someone paying attention. A woman pouring milk, a chair, a pond at noon. This season we looked at how Europe’s old masters still teach us to slow down, and why their quiet ideas feel so new.

— The editors

I

On light

Slow living, garden light

Monet began building his water garden at Giverny in 1893 and went on to paint its lilies for some thirty years, around 250 canvases in all. The subject was never the flower. It was the light moving across the surface of the water, which is why slow, patient attention is the real trend hiding in these pictures.

Look for

  • There is almost no horizon: the pond becomes the whole picture.
  • Sky and clouds appear as reflections, not above the water.
  • The edges of the lilies dissolve into short, broken strokes.

A thought that runs through this issue

A short map of European painting

Eight turning points, from the Renaissance to the first abstract pictures. Each one changed what a painting was allowed to be. Scroll, and the painting follows.

01/ 08

  1. c. 1485

    Renaissance

    Painters study anatomy and linear perspective, and bring classical myth back to life. Botticelli’s Birth of Venus shows the ideal body in an idealised world.

    Sandro Botticelli · Uffizi, Florence
  2. 1642

    Baroque & the Dutch Golden Age

    Drama, deep shadow and a new market: in the Netherlands, middle-class buyers want portraits, still lifes and scenes of daily life. Rembrandt paints a civic militia as a theatre scene.

    Rembrandt van Rijn · Rijksmuseum, Amsterdam
  3. 1830

    Romanticism

    Feeling over reason. Delacroix turns the July Revolution into a painting of passion, with Liberty striding over the barricade, flag in hand.

    Eugène Delacroix · Louvre, Paris
  4. 1876

    Impressionism

    Paint outdoors, quickly, and record the light of a passing moment. The name came from a critic mocking Monet’s Impression, Sunrise at the group’s first exhibition in 1874.

    Pierre-Auguste Renoir · Musée d’Orsay, Paris
  5. 1893

    Post-Impressionism

    Colour and structure become the subject. Cézanne tilts the table to show several viewpoints at once, opening the road to Cubism, while Van Gogh’s paint turns a night sky into an emotion.

    Paul Cézanne · Art Institute of Chicago
  6. 1894

    Art Nouveau & the Secession

    Decoration becomes serious art: flowing lines, flat pattern, gold. Mucha’s poster for Sarah Bernhardt launched the style in Paris, and the Vienna Secession followed in 1897.

    Alphonse Mucha · colour lithograph poster, Paris
  7. 1912

    Expressionism

    Distortion in service of inner states. Schiele’s tense, angular self-portraits show a person rather than a pose.

    Egon Schiele · Leopold Museum, Vienna
  8. 1913

    Abstraction

    Kandinsky argues that colour and form can move us like music, without depicting anything at all.

    Wassily Kandinsky · Tretyakov Gallery, Moscow

Five trends,
quietly returning

What European painters noticed centuries ago is shaping how we dress, decorate and spend our weekends. Hover to see each painting and what it means.

  • 01Slow livingGardens · CalmVermeerThe Milkmaid · Johannes Vermeer, c. 1657–58 · Rijksmuseum, Amsterdam · Wikimedia Commons

    Nothing happens here: a servant pours milk. Vermeer gives that small task the dignity of a saint’s portrait, painting the bread, the thin stream and the window light with equal care. The deeper meaning is that ordinary work, done attentively, is worth looking at. That is slow living in a single frame.

  • 02The romantic revivalCottagecore · FashionWaterhouseThe Lady of Shalott · John William Waterhouse, 1888 · Tate Britain, London · Wikimedia Commons

    In Tennyson’s poem, a woman cursed to see the world only in a mirror looks at Lancelot directly and the curse falls. Waterhouse paints her as she floats toward Camelot, knowing the cost, with three candles guttering beside her. She chooses life over safety, and that bittersweet mood is why her flowing hair and autumn woods keep returning in fashion and photography.

  • 03Immersive exhibitionsLight · DigitalMonetImpression, Sunrise · Claude Monet, 1872 · Musée Marmottan Monet, Paris · Wikimedia Commons

    Monet painted the port of Le Havre at dawn in a few loose strokes: an orange sun, grey-blue mist, boats as silhouettes. Critics mocked it as unfinished and coined the word “Impressionism”. Its argument, that the feeling of a moment matters more than an exact record, is why immersive shows now surround visitors with light instead of hanging a single frame.

  • 04Gold & ornamentDesign · InteriorsKlimtPortrait of Adele Bloch-Bauer I · Gustav Klimt, 1907 · Neue Galerie, New York · Wikimedia Commons

    Adele, a patron of Vienna’s artistic circle, almost dissolves into a field of gold leaf, eyes and spirals echoing Byzantine mosaics and Egyptian motifs. Only her face and hands are naturalistic; everything else is ornament. The picture asks whether decoration can be a kind of portrait, and why gold still signals both luxury and reverence.

  • 05Bold geometryColour · GraphicMalevichSuprematist Composition · Kazimir Malevich, 1916 · Stedelijk Museum, Amsterdam · Wikimedia Commons

    Malevich dropped the object altogether. Rectangles drift across white space with no ground and no horizon, in what he called the supremacy of pure feeling. The picture proposed that colour and shape alone can carry emotion, which is the root of the bold geometry now seen on posters, ceramics and interiors.

A field guide

Five minutes with a painting

  1. Stand back, then move close.Take in the whole composition first. Then step in until you can see the brushwork and the cracks in the paint.
  2. Find the light.Where does it come from? In Millais’s Ophelia, every leaf is lit evenly, which makes the scene feel both real and dreamlike.
  3. Follow the eyes.Whom is the painter asking you to look at, and what is just outside the edge of the frame?
  4. Count the colours you can name.Then count the ones you cannot. Millais painted this river from life, over months, to get its greens right.
  5. Read the label last.Decide what you feel before you learn the title, the date and the story.

05  Museums

Rooms made for looking slowly

LouvreParis, France

Home of the Mona Lisa and the Winged Victory. Pick one wing, not the whole building.

RijksmuseumAmsterdam, Netherlands

The Gallery of Honour leads to Rembrandt’s Night Watch, in a building renovated and reopened in 2013.

PradoMadrid, Spain

Velázquez’s Las Meninas and Goya’s dark late paintings. Worth an afternoon in a single room.

UffiziFlorence, Italy

Botticelli’s Birth of Venus and Primavera, where the Renaissance began.

Visiting tip
Book timed tickets online, go on a weekday afternoon, and choose one room to stay in. Ten paintings seen slowly beat a hundred seen in a rush.

06  Artists

Three self-portraits

Self-Portrait · Vincent van Gogh, 1889 · Musée d’Orsay, Paris · Wikimedia Commons, public domain

Vincent van Gogh

Netherlands · 1853–1890

He painted for only about ten years yet left roughly 2,100 works, and sold very few in his lifetime. Most of his best-known paintings date from his last two years in southern France.

Self-portrait as the Allegory of Painting · Artemisia Gentileschi, c. 1638–39 · Royal Collection Trust, Windsor · Wikimedia Commons, public domain

Artemisia Gentileschi

Italy · 1593–c. 1656

The first woman admitted to the Accademia delle Arti del Disegno in Florence, in 1616. Her heroines, such as Judith, are painted with unflinching force.

Self-Portrait · Rembrandt van Rijn, 1659 · National Gallery of Art, Washington · Wikimedia Commons, public domain

Rembrandt van Rijn

Netherlands · 1606–1669

He made around eighty self-portraits in paint, etching and drawing, a lifelong diary of his own face from bold young man to weathered old one.

07  Glossary

Six words to know

01  The Magdalen with the Smoking Flame · Georges de La Tour, c. 1640 · LACMA, Los Angeles · Wikimedia Commons, public domain

Chiaroscuro

Strong contrast of light and dark used to model form. Caravaggio and La Tour made candlelight their signature.

02  Woman with a Parasol · Claude Monet, 1875 · National Gallery of Art, Washington · Wikimedia Commons, public domain

Plein air

Painting outdoors from direct observation, made practical in the 1840s by paint sold in portable tubes.

03  Wheatfield with Crows · Vincent van Gogh, 1890 · Van Gogh Museum, Amsterdam · Wikimedia Commons, public domain

Impasto

Paint laid on so thickly that the brushstrokes stand out in relief, as in Van Gogh’s last wheatfields.

04  A Sunday on La Grande Jatte · Georges Seurat, 1884–86 · Art Institute of Chicago · Wikimedia Commons, public domain

Pointillism

Tiny dots of unmixed colour that blend in the viewer’s eye, developed by Seurat in the 1880s.

05  Judith I · Gustav Klimt, 1901 · Belvedere, Vienna · Wikimedia Commons, public domain

Secession

A break with the official academies. The Vienna Secession of 1897 included Klimt, who painted Judith in 1901.

06  Malle Babbe · Frans Hals, c. 1633–35 · Gemäldegalerie, Berlin · Wikimedia Commons, public domain

Tronie

A Dutch seventeenth-century study of a face or character type, not a portrait of a named person.
